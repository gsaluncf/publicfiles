The business value of data

An orchestra can count how many first-time ticket buyers come back for a second concert. Its marketing director also needs to know whether sending an invitation brings in enough additional ticket revenue to cover the cost. Those questions require different comparisons.

Business priorities

Thomas Davenport and Jeanne Harris describe companies that invest in analysis of a few activities central to their success. A retailer might use sales records to set prices or decide how much inventory to order. An orchestra might use ticket records to identify which buyers return.

For the Oakland Athletics, the priority was finding hitters the team could afford. On-base percentage helped managers identify players who reached base often. Managers could then compare each player’s performance with the salary the team would have to pay him.

Thomas H. Davenport and Jeanne G. Harris, Competing on Analytics (2007), book excerptMoneyball and player salaries
02

Comparing possible actions

Beth Stackpole describes how managers can choose an analysis by first naming the actions available to them. An orchestra’s marketing director could send first-time buyers an invitation to a second concert. To test the offer, staff could randomly assign buyers to two groups. Both groups would receive the orchestra’s usual communications. Only one group would receive the extra invitation. Staff could then compare the percentage in each group who bought another ticket over the same period.

The eBay researchers made a similar comparison when they tested paid search ads. They estimated that each dollar spent on ads for search terms without the eBay name produced about $0.37 in additional revenue. The finance manager could compare that estimate with the cost of buying the ads.

Beth Stackpole, MIT Sloan, Decisions, not data, should drive analytics programs (2021)eBay and paid search advertising
03

The cost of analysis

Rasmus Wegener and Velu Sinha at Bain identify four parts of an investment in analytics. An organization needs reliable data, tools to analyze the data, people with the skills to do the work, and managers who use the results.

For an invitation test, box office staff need accurate purchase dates and customer records. An analyst needs access to the ticketing system and time to match invitations to purchases. Finance staff can estimate the additional ticket revenue after discounts. They can then subtract the cost of preparing the analysis, sending the invitations, and serving the additional customers.

Rasmus Wegener and Velu Sinha, Bain, The value of Big Data: How analytics differentiates winners (2013)Theatre Royal Plymouth’s membership campaign
04

Generalists and specialists

Sara Brown describes how organizations divide analytical work. A generalist analyst can prepare data, perform an analysis, and explain the results. On a team of specialists, a data engineer might maintain the records that a data scientist uses to estimate the effect of a campaign.

One data science graduate at an orchestra could check the ticket records, calculate what percentage of buyers in each group bought another ticket, and discuss the results with the box office manager. If several departments need the same records each week, a data engineer could automate the updates. The analyst would then have more time to investigate differences in attendance.

Sara Brown, MIT Sloan, How to build a data analytics dream team (2020)
05

Responsibilities across departments

McKinsey describes three ways to organize analysts. They can work together in a central department, work within individual business units, or divide responsibilities between a central team and those units. In each arrangement, analysts need regular contact with the people who will act on their findings.

At an orchestra, the box office manager could define which purchases count as a return visit. The analyst could report the difference between the two groups. Finance staff could calculate the contribution from additional sales after discounts and direct costs. The marketing director would use those results to decide whether to send the next invitation.

McKinsey, Building an effective analytics organization (2018)
