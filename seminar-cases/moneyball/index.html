Moneyball and player salaries

The Oakland Athletics had a limited budget for player salaries. The team’s managers looked for affordable hitters who reached base often.

01

Batting records and team payrolls

Oakland’s managers used on-base percentage to measure how often a hitter reached base. A batter who walks reaches first base. Oakland’s coaches encouraged hitters to let pitches outside the strike zone go by. Economists Jahn Hakes and Raymond Sauer compared payrolls and winning percentages across teams. From 1999 to 2003, Oakland won a higher share of its games than many teams with larger payrolls. Hakes and Sauer, pp. 181 to 182.

02

Salary negotiations

A player and his team generally agree on a salary before the season begins. Hakes and Sauer compared each player’s salary with his batting statistics from the previous season. They also accounted for whether he could negotiate with other teams as a free agent or seek salary arbitration. Salary analysis, pp. 177 to 178.

03

Batting average and on-base percentage

Batting average measures hits per at-bat. On-base percentage (OBP) counts three ways a batter can reach base. Its numerator includes hits, walks, and hit-by-pitches. Its denominator includes at-bats, walks, hit-by-pitches, and sacrifice flies.

The 2002 records for Scott Hatteberg and Miguel Tejada list at-bats (AB), hits (H), walks (BB), hit-by-pitches (HBP), and sacrifice flies (SF). Major League Baseball’s definition of on-base percentage.

04

On-base percentage and salary

Hakes and Sauer found a statistically significant association between higher on-base percentages and higher salaries in 2004. The annual estimates for 2000 through 2003 were not statistically significant. Table 3 and explanation, pp. 179 to 180.
